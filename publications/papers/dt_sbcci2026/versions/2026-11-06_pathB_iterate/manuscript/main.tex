\small\noindent\textbf{\textit{Abstract}---}Aging sensors validated under burn-in feed silicon lifecycle management (SLM), yet burn-in benches control the chamber, not the die the sensor sees. On one FPGA and slack sensor, we compare a stock thermostatic oven without die feedback and a die-referenced PID oven. The thermostat delivered \vAFratio$\times$ less Arrhenius acceleration (\vAFratioSPadj$\times$ at matched air setpoint), through mean die-temperature shortfall rather than ripple, and left \vSigRawRatio$\times$ more residual sensor noise. \noindent On-chip aging sensors are moving from research prototypes into the silicon lifecycle management (SLM) stack, where their readings drive adaptive voltage scaling (AVS), remaining-useful-life estimation, and maintenance decisions~\cite{kashyap,niemann}. Before a sensor is trusted in the field, it is usually validated under accelerated burn-in (here, HTOL-like aging stress, not infant-mortality screening): the device is heated and over-volted, and the sensor is expected to report the resulting delay degradation. FPGA aging sensors have been validated this way for over a decade~\cite{valdes}.

Stress standards anchor it differently. JEDEC JESD22-A108 sets operating-life stress by a minimum junction temperature, adjusting ambient and bias to reach it, and leaves the detailed application of burn-in outside its scope~\cite{jedec}; MIL-STD-883 Method~1015 sets burn-in by ambient temperature, with the junction as an upper bound~\cite{mil}. Either way, a typical bench controls and logs the \emph{chamber} and infers the junction from ambient, bias and thermal resistance. Self-heating that lifts the junction above the chamber is well known in industrial burn-in, where scaling drove per-device thermal management~\cite{semenov}, and a dedicated FPGA aging bench regulates die temperature and internal voltage precisely because its on-chip ring oscillators respond to both~\cite{sobas}. What has not been quantified is the cost, in delivered stress, residual noise and detection time, of the chamber-referenced benches many labs still use to \emph{validate on-chip sensors}, which sample the die rather than the chamber. This matters because a sensor's own aging already shifts its calibration~\cite{anik}; unreported bench stress and noise make sensor and bench harder still to separate.

Our SBCCI~2026 paper~\cite{sbcci26} compared a thermostatic (bang-bang) oven and a closed-loop PID oven on the same FPGA and sensor and found that, as operated (only the PID used die feedback), the two benches delivered different stress and different reversible noise. This article asks the practical follow-up: \emph{how long must a burn-in run last before a sensor can see aging, and how much does the bench change that time?} Answering it needs the induced aging rate and injected noise, which a 23-hour campaign cannot provide.

The argument is a chain: the bench sets the die-temperature trajectory; its mean fixes the stress delivered and hence the aging signal, its ripple sets the reversible noise, and signal and noise together fix how long a burn-in must run before the sensor sees aging, a limit every SLM function trained on the result inherits. Beyond the conference version, the main contributions of this work are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A source decomposition of the delivered-stress bias.} The \vAFratio$\times$ acceleration-factor gap is set by the mean die-temperature shortfall; thermostat ripple contributes \vRippleBB\%, as a second-order expansion predicts; hence a die-level tracking budget.
\item \textbf{A \vDurRCA-hour sustained-stress campaign} on a second monitored circuit, resolving a sublinear slack drift after PVT correction.
\item \textbf{Detection time, three ways.} A white-noise bound, a long-run-variance estimate and a sequential test, which differ because the residual wanders slowly and aging is sublinear.
\item \textbf{A measured bench penalty on detectability.} Noise and decorrelation are measured on the paired runs; the aging law converting acceleration into drift rate is bracketed.
\item \textbf{A reproducible, commodity PID bench}, documented from plant identification to tuning and validated against the stock thermostat at a second setpoint.
\item \textbf{A five-item reporting checklist} for any 1-Hz burn-in log.
\end{enumerate}

In short, the bench's mean die temperature, not its ripple, sets the stress; its noise and that stress together set a detection time of about \vTminLrv\,h on the PID bench (\vTminSeq\,h as a descriptive lower bound), which a thermostatic bench would lengthen.

\section{Instrument, benches, and methods}

\textbf{Sensor and DUT.} The DUT is a 28\,nm Xilinx Artix-7 FPGA on a Nexys~4~DDR board; every campaign below ran on the same board. The instrument is our auto-tuning slack sensor~\cite{sbcci25}: an MMCM dynamic phase shift advances a sampling clock in fixed steps until an XOR comparator detects that the monitored path's output has not yet settled, and the number of steps is reported as the path's slack in counts (cnt). The step is $1/(56f_{\mathrm{VCO}})$: \vPsINV\,ps in the inverter-chain design of the paired runs~\cite{sbcci26} and \vPsRCA\,ps in the bitstream of the long run. The sensor recalibrates autonomously~\cite{sbcci25}; in the long run's bitstream every reading is a fresh sweep from the same restored phase, with no stored baseline, so recalibration does not subtract slow drift. Die temperature and core voltage $V_{\mathrm{CCINT}}$ come from the on-chip XADC. Its absolute offset is common to all runs, so temperature differences are robust, but errors against the die target carry its absolute accuracy of a few degrees. A supervisory PC logs slack, die temperature and voltage, with oven temperature and supply current where available; time constants are computed in seconds from each log's timestamps. The stress (1.3--1.4\,V core, die near 125\degC) exceeds the device's datasheet absolute-maximum core voltage and commercial junction range: it is accelerated overstress, chosen to make drift observable within days.

\textbf{Two benches.} Both use the same 30-liter laboratory drying oven. The \emph{bang-bang} bench keeps the oven's stock thermostat, the arrangement typical of academic aging labs, with a rate-limited software loop (5\,mV steps, at least 2\,s apart) that holds the mean supply voltage but not the thermal cycle. The \emph{PID} bench replaces the thermostat with a microcontroller driving a zero-crossing solid-state relay (SSR) in a 5\,s window. A 50\% open-loop power step from \vFopdtTzero\degC{} identified a first-order-plus-dead-time model with gain \vFopdtK\degC{} per percent, dead time \vFopdtTheta\,s, and time constant \vFopdtTau\,s. Skogestad's SIMC rules~\cite{simc} gave the starting gains ($K_p=\vSimcKp$, $K_i=\vSimcKiE\times10^{-3}\unit{s^{-1}}$). Because no first-order model captures the oven's overshoot, the gains were refined on the oven, lowering the integral gain and adding a derivative term ($K_p=\vFinalKp$, $K_i=\vFinalKiE\times10^{-3}\unit{s^{-1}}$, $K_d=\vFinalKd\unit{s}$), plus a soft-start ramp. The parts are commodity: a Cortex-M0+ board, an NTC thermistor, the SSR and a SCPI supply. An outer loop moves the oven setpoint in 1\degC{} steps to hold the die target, using the XADC reading. The thermostatic bench has no die feedback. The comparison is therefore between two complete bench architectures as labs deploy them, not between two controllers on an otherwise identical loop.

\textbf{Is the PID a fair baseline?} The thermostatic oven is not a strawman: it is the unmodified commercial chamber. Tuning matters within PID too: at a 100\degC{} oven-air setpoint, the SIMC starting gains left \vSdSimc\degC{} steady-state standard deviation and \vOverSimc\degC{} overshoot, against \vSdTuned\degC{} and \vOverTuned\degC{} after refinement.

\textbf{Evidence base.} Table~\ref{tab:evidence} lists the campaigns. The two \vDurPair-hour runs share the die target (115\degC), the on-die voltage (1.3\,V) and the monitored circuit, a 50-stage inverter chain~\cite{sbcci26}. After the soak hour, the PID outer loop held the oven air at \vPIDspMax\degC{} and the PID supply stayed at a fixed \vVpsuPID\,V, whereas the bang-bang bench trimmed its supply in software. The runs were acquired in separate campaigns~\cite{sbcci26}: the bang-bang run, logged every \vCadBB\,s, is the first day of the Week-1 burn-in of~\cite{sbcci25} (April 2025), and the PID run came in January 2026. The \vDurRCA-hour PID campaign, first reported here, monitors a different circuit, a 16-bit ripple-carry adder, at 125\degC{} die target (\vTRCA\degC{} mean, \vTsdRCA\degC{} standard deviation) and 1.4\,V on-die, held by the software trim with the supply between \vPSUminRCA{} and \vPSUmaxRCA\,V. Because the circuits differ, only dimensionless quantities (acceleration-factor and noise ratios) are carried between them. As in~\cite{sbcci26}, stress-delivery statistics cover each whole run, while regression, noise and detectability statistics exclude the first hour as thermal soak.

\textbf{Analysis methods.} Slack $S$ is regressed by least squares on die temperature and voltage; its residual is the PVT-corrected slack. The \emph{residual noise} $\sigma$ is that residual's standard deviation, and its \emph{fast} part is what remains after a one-hour moving average is removed. The drift $\beta$ is the least-squares slope of the corrected slack versus time. Its uncertainty uses Newey--West errors for serial correlation, with bandwidth up to 8\,h on long runs. The integrated autocorrelation time $\tint=\tfrac{1}{2}+\sum_k\rho_k$ (in time units, Sokal windowing) measures how quickly the fast residual decorrelates. In noise of variance $\sigma^2$ and correlation time $\tint$, a least-squares slope over duration $T$ has asymptotic variance $24\,\sigma^2\tint/T^3$, so a drift $\beta$ exceeds three standard errors after
\begin{equation}
\Tmin=\left(\frac{216\,\sigma^2\,\tint}{\beta^2}\right)^{1/3}.
\label{eq:tmin}
\end{equation}
This is the trend-detection time of environmental statistics~\cite{weatherhead}, with $2\tint$ as the noise-persistence factor: $\Tmin$ grows only as $\sigma^{2/3}$, because a slope's error shrinks as $T^{-3/2}$. Because $2\sigma^2\tint$ is the residual's long-run variance only if it decorrelates within $\tint$, we also measure the long-run variance directly from block means over increasing averaging times and substitute it in Eq.~\eqref{eq:tmin}. A third, empirical estimate tracks the cumulative slope's significance as the run grows. For alarms, we model a threshold detector that compares two adjacent windows of length $w$ as a binary symmetric channel with crossover probability $p=Q(\mathrm{SNR}_w/2)$, $\mathrm{SNR}_w=\beta w/(\sigma\sqrt{4\tint/w})$, and capacity $C_{\mathrm{BSC}}=1-H_2(p)$.

\section{Delivered stress: The mean, not the ripple}

Burn-in accelerates bias-temperature instability and related mechanisms according to the Arrhenius relation, and for a time-varying die temperature the stress actually delivered is the trajectory average
\begin{equation}
\langle AF\rangle=\Big\langle \exp\!\Big[\frac{E_a}{k_B}\Big(\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{use}}}-\frac{1}{T_j(t)}\Big)\Big]\Big\rangle_t .
\label{eq:af}
\end{equation}
At the same die-temperature target, the thermostatic bench held the die at \vTBB\degC{} on average and the PID bench at \vTPID\degC. At $E_a=0.7\unit{eV}$ and $T_{\mathrm{use}}=55$\degC{} this gives $\langle AF\rangle=\vAFBB$ and \vAFPID: relative to the PID bench, whose die ran \vTerrPID\degC{} above target, the thermostatic bench delivered \vAFratio$\times$ less acceleration (\vEqhBB{} versus \vEqhPID{} equivalent hours at the mean die temperature~\cite{sbcci26}). In~\cite{sbcci26} we related the shortfall to thermostatic cycling coupling with die self-heating~\cite{semenov}. The PID bench's die-referenced setpoint trim contributes as well: it held the air \vSPoffset\degC{} above the thermostat's nominal 80\degC{} dial. Shifting the thermostatic die trajectory by that offset leaves a setpoint-adjusted ratio of \vAFratioSPadj$\times$, assuming that the die follows the air one-for-one and that the thermostat held its dial, neither testable without an oven-air log; as self-heating amplifies the air's effect on the die, this is likely an upper bound. We therefore report the \vAFratio$\times$ gap as a property of the two benches as operated.

A common intuition is that ripple distorts the stress, since the exponential in Eq.~\eqref{eq:af} weights hot excursions more than cold ones. The measured trajectories contradict it. Ripple raises the thermostatic bench's delivered AF above that of its mean temperature by only \vRippleBB\% (PID: \vRipplePID\%), although its die-temperature standard deviation is \vTsdRatio$\times$ larger (\vTsdBB{} vs.\ \vTsdPID\degC). A second-order (Jensen) expansion of Eq.~\eqref{eq:af} about the mean predicts a gain of $\tfrac{1}{2}(E_a\sigma_T/k_BT^2)^2$, \vJensen\%: each kelvin moves AF by only about \vAFperK\%, so ripple averages out to second order, whereas a mean error compounds at first order. The mean shortfall alone predicts a \vAFratioMeanOnly$\times$ ratio, slightly more than the full \vAFratio$\times$: ripple mildly \emph{offsets} the bias [Figure~\ref{fig:stress}(a)]. Over $E_a\in[0.5,0.9]$\,eV the ratio spans about \vAFratioLoOne--\vAFratioHiOne$\times$, so its direction does not depend on the mechanism assumed.

A lab therefore cannot repair a thermostatic bench by tightening hysteresis; it must regulate the die's mean temperature, which requires die telemetry used in closed loop. A first-order expansion of Eq.~\eqref{eq:af} gives the mean die-temperature error $\delta$ that keeps the delivered AF within a fraction $\eta$ of nominal,
\begin{equation}
\delta\approx\frac{k_B T_{\mathrm{set}}^2}{E_a}\,\ln(1+\eta),
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
with $T_{\mathrm{set}}$ in kelvin. At 115\degC{} and 0.7\,eV, a $\pm$5\%, $\pm$10\%, or $\pm$20\% AF tolerance allows only \vDeltaFive, \vDeltaTen, or \vDeltaTwenty\degC. The bang-bang die shortfall of \vTerrBB\degC{} (an AF deviation of \vAFdevBB\%) exceeds even the 20\% budget twofold, while the PID die overshoot of \vTerrPID\degC{} ($+$\vAFdevPID\%) is small, stable, and visible [Figure~\ref{fig:stress}(b)].

The effect is not tied to one setpoint. At 100\degC{} oven air, the stock thermostat held a \vSdThermo\degC{} standard deviation, \vPtpThermo\degC{} peak-to-peak swing and \vEmaxThermo\degC{} maximum error over the last 30 minutes, against \vSdTuned, \vPtpTuned{} and \vEmaxTuned\degC{} under PID. These chamber-air figures are what a bench usually logs; the die data show that the air ripple was the visible symptom, the mean die shortfall the consequential error.

\section{Reversible noise: What the bench writes into the sensor}

The slack a sensor reports mixes a slow, permanent aging term with a fast, reversible PVT term. In the \vDurPair-hour pair, die temperature and voltage linearly explain \vRsqBB\% of slack variance under bang-bang and \vRsqPID\% under PID. A lower $R^2$ under PID is not lower fidelity: the controlled bench simply injects less environmental variance to explain. What matters downstream is the noise left after correction: the residual of the joint temperature--voltage fit is \vSigRawBB{} cnt under bang-bang and \vSigRawPID{} cnt under PID~\cite{sbcci26}, a \vSigRawRatio$\times$ higher noise floor for any AVS or alarm calibrated on the thermostatic bench~\cite{niemann}; on the fast residual, which is robust to early drift on the then-unstressed device, the ratio is \vSigRatio$\times$. The on-die voltage also varied more under bang-bang (\vVsdBB{} versus \vVsdPID\,mV standard deviation); only that bench trimmed its supply, and its 5\,mV steps may add to the die leakage that follows the thermal cycle. These short-term values are representative: the fast residual of the \vDurBBlong-hour Week-1 log is \vBBlongSigRatio{} times its first day's, and that of the long PID run's first day \vRCAfirstSigRatio{} times the whole run's.

Two candidate mechanisms fail. Thermal lag between the XADC and the path would make slack hysteretic, but the heating-minus-cooling residual gap is \vHystBB\,cnt (\vHystPct\% of the residual) and a $dT/dt$ regressor lowers the residual by \vDtdtGainPct\%. Nonlinear coupling would survive linear correction, but on the long run a linear correction removes \vRCAMIremoved\% of the short-term mutual information, and residual-MI comparisons between benches are limited by integer slack quantization. The thermostatic excess behaves as broadband variance.

\section{Detectability: When can a sensor see aging?}

\textbf{A drift is resolved, and it is sublinear.} After PVT correction, the \vDurRCA-hour residual drifts at \vBetaRCA\mcnth{} on average (95\% interval \vBetaLoRCA{} to \vBetaHiRCA; correlation with time \vRCAcorrStRaw{} before correction, \vRCAcorrStCorr{} after). It slows from \vRCAfirstHalf{} to \vRCAsecondHalf\mcnth{} between the two halves of the run, and a power law $t^{n}$ with $n=\vPowNRCA$ fits far better than a line ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=\vdBICblockRCA$ on 2-hour block means), consistent with bias-temperature instability. The total is \vRCAdriftCnt\,cnt, about \vRCAdriftPs\,ps of lost slack. We call it a drift, not aging: no logged channel can serve as a readout control, and the outer loop moved the oven setpoint \vRCAspChanges{} times (\vRCAspMin--\vRCAspMax\degC); \vSPjumpFrac\% of these moves coincide with a residual step beyond the 95th percentile at random times (5\% expected), a modest source of slow residual wander. The fitted exponent is apparent: time counts from this run's start on a device with prior stress history. The slope depends on the regressor set: adding oven temperature gives \vBetaRegOven\mcnth; adding the supply voltage, the trim loop's actuator, which may absorb aging signal, gives \vBetaRegPsu\mcnth; the joint fit $S\sim T+V+t$ gives \vBetaJoint\mcnth. All are negative.

\textbf{The minimum burn-in time.} With $\sigma=\vSigRCA$\,cnt and $\tint=\vTauRCA$\,s for the fast residual (the full residual about the trend is \vSigFullRCA\,cnt) and the average $\beta$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:tmin} gives $\Tmin=\vTminBound$\,h. The residual does not decorrelate within $\tint$, however: its long-run variance keeps growing with averaging time, \vRCAlrvGrowth-fold from 15-minute to 8-hour blocks, and the 8-hour value raises $\Tmin$ to \vTminLrv\,h, our defensible estimate. Because the drift is fastest early, the cumulative slope stays beyond 3$\sigma$ from \vTminSeq\,h on [Figure~\ref{fig:detect}(a)], but this is descriptive and a lower bound: repeated looks inflate significance, and slopes over \vWinLen-hour windows (2-hour stride) range from \vWinSlopeMin{} to $+$\vWinSlopeMax\mcnth, \vWinPosPct\% of them positive. Table~\ref{tab:tmin} collects the three. Hence the 23-hour runs, whose late-window slopes SBCCI~2026 found nonzero~\cite{sbcci26}, cannot alone decide whether a slope is aging.

\textbf{The bench penalty.} By Eq.~\eqref{eq:tmin} the bench enters through the noise $\sigma$ and decorrelation $\tint$ it injects and the stress that sets $\beta$. On the paired runs the thermostatic bench raises $\sigma$ by \vSigRawRatio$\times$; at a common sampling interval both inverter-chain residuals are close to white ($\tint$ of \vTauBBs{} and \vTauPIDs\,s), unlike the adder's. The stress enters through the aging law. If drift rate scales with delivered AF, the detection-time stress penalty is $\vAFratio^{2/3}=\vPenStress\times$; if drift follows the fitted power law in equivalent time, $(AF\,t)^{\vNLaw}$, it is only \vPenStressPow$\times$. With the noise penalty of $\vSigRawRatio^{2/3}=\vPenNoiseSigOnly\times$, a thermostatic bench would need a projected \vPenLow--\vPenMid$\times$ longer, or \vPenHigh$\times$ with the measured $\tint$ ratio (Table~\ref{tab:tmin}); after \vDurPairPost\,h it would sit at \vSeqZbb$\sigma$, on the threshold, where the PID run already stood at \vSeqZpid$\sigma$. The projection assumes that the inverter-chain noise ratio transfers to the adder, which these data cannot test. Yet the thermostatic Week-1 log resolved its drift after \vTminSeqBBlong\,h [Figure~\ref{fig:detect}(a)]: on an unstressed device, early aging shows through the thermostat's noise, so detection time depends on aging state as much as on the bench.

\textbf{Alarm reliability.} SLM functions consume decisions, not slopes~\cite{kashyap}. Under white noise a 24-hour alarm errs with probability \vPerrPidBound\% on the PID run (\vCpidBound\,bits per decision) and \vPerrBbBound\% (\vCbbBound) projected for the thermostat; with the measured slow wander, \vPerrPidLrv\% and \vPerrBbLrv\% [Figure~\ref{fig:detect}(b)]. A window chosen on one bench is mis-set on the other, adding missed and false alarms to those a sensor's own calibration drift causes~\cite{anik}.

\textbf{From detectability to failure.} A flip-flop failure catcher at the end of the monitored path records a real timing violation at the sensor-reported failure point, within clock-jitter margin~\cite{sbcci25}, so slack has a physical zero and a detected drift becomes a distance to failure under stress. We do not extrapolate a lifetime from one board and one operating point.

\section{What labs should report}

JEDEC already defines operating-life stress at the junction and has the test specification state chamber ambient and maximum junction temperature~\cite{jedec}; MIL-STD-883 sets the ambient~\cite{mil}. Either way the bench controls the air, and the gap is in verification. A bench that logs only the chamber cannot show the stress and noise reaching the powered die a sensor samples. For sensor-instrumented burn-in we propose the five-item report of Table~\ref{tab:checklist}, which adds to A108's items the die mean and trajectory, $\sigma$, $\tint$ and $\Tmin$. Each item is computable from a standard 1-Hz log, and together they let a reader judge whether a published aging curve reflects the device or the bench. Die-level regulation is achievable~\cite{sobas}, yet in sensor validations under burn-in the delivered die trajectory and $\Tmin$ are not routinely reported; in our data they change the conclusions most.

\textbf{Limitations.} All data come from one 28\,nm board, so bench effects are established within one device, not as population statistics. The two comparisons use different monitored circuits, and the paired runs were separate campaigns at different points in the device's stress history, with different oven setpoints and supply control; these confounders bear on the stress and noise ratios. The thermostatic detectability figures are projections. No logged channel isolates readout drift. Because the stress is overstress, mechanisms beyond field-representative aging may contribute; the findings concern the bench, not the device's lifetime. We do not claim compliance with either standard~\cite{jedec,mil}.

\section{Conclusion}

The question this article posed has a measured answer on the PID bench, a detection time of about \vTminLrv\,h under the measured slow noise (\vTminSeq\,h as a descriptive lower bound), and a projected one for the thermostatic bench, \vPenLow--\vPenHigh$\times$ longer. The lesson for anyone validating an on-chip sensor under burn-in is that the bench belongs in the measurement chain. In our pair, as operated without die feedback, the thermostatic bench's dominant error was the mean die temperature, not the air ripple a chamber log shows; it logged the die but did not regulate it. That error reached the sensor twice: as a \vAFratio$\times$ shortfall in delivered stress (\vAFratioSPadj$\times$ at a matched air setpoint) and as a \vSigRawRatio$\times$ higher residual noise floor. Because detection time also depends on the device's aging state, the checklist asks for the method behind any reported $\Tmin$, not only its value.

Three extensions follow directly. A matched-duration thermostatic campaign on the same circuit would replace the projection with a measurement; multi-device replication would yield population statistics; and a 16\,nm FinFET platform, where published drifts are below 0.1\% over 1,000 hours even at elevated stress~\cite{sobas}, will test whether the bench-set detection floor tightens as expected. Sensor-validated burn-in now feeds SLM, so these benches should be reported with the same care as the sensors they validate.
